11The Age of Intelligence

Artificial intelligence should remain a tool of discernment, not a substitute for human responsibility.

Reading approximately 20–30 minVideo 5:05 · optionalGroup optional

The full text is the core of the chapter. The video and group work are optional extensions.

In this chapter, you will learn to
  • name the opportunities and risks of generative AI in theology and education
  • protect the authorial voice, privacy, truthfulness and the verification of sources
  • set up a transparent workflow in which the decision remains with the human person.
Chapter videoAIVideo in Slovak

We live in the age of artificial intelligence. Language today evolves in the digital space as well. And into that space enter new tools that change both our thinking and our relationships. Artificial intelligence can help, but it can also tempt us into shortcuts. If we want to walk together, we face the challenge of learning to use these tools responsibly, too.

This book has been accompanied by an experience that is still relatively new for the academic world: generative AI has entered the work with text. Not as an author, but as a tool. In theology this raises legitimate questions: What does it do to truth, authorship, argumentation and responsibility?

AI can simplify, speed things up and help sort material. At the same time, however, it can create an illusion of certainty and generate inaccuracies. Working with AI therefore also becomes a moral question: How do we use the tool so that it does not damage trust in theological work?

This chapter presents AI as another "sign of the times" and, at the same time, as a practical tool: how to work with it in theology and in mediation without a person losing their own voice.

Dialogue

Without Losing One's Own Voice

01

What is the greatest risk of working with generative AI in theology?

The greatest risk is false certainty. A text can sound convincing even when it is factually wrong. And in theology this can turn into a "pious" error that spreads quickly.

The second risk is the loss of voice: when a person is carried away by the smooth style and stops discerning whether they really mean it that way.

02

And what is the greatest opportunity?

The opportunity lies in working with a large amount of material: interview transcripts, notes, conference texts. AI can help sort, summarise, propose structure, find repetitions and gaps.

Another opportunity is didactic: helping students learn to argue, to ask questions and to discern sources.

03

How does one stay honest?

Honesty means three things: transparency about where AI helped, verification of facts and authorial responsibility. A text is signed by a person, not a tool.

If AI "makes something up", the responsibility lies with the user who accepted the output uncritically or failed to verify it.

04

Can AI also help in mediation or counselling?

It can help with preparation: proposing questions, a meeting framework, or summarising the parties' positions. It cannot, however, replace human presence. In mediation, what is crucial is perceiving the emotions, the tension and the non-verbal communication of the other person, as well as one's own bodily and emotional reactions. That is human work.

In counselling it is even more sensitive: AI can offer language, but it cannot carry a relationship.

05

How does ethics enter into this?

Ethics enters into the protection of privacy, the handling of sensitive data, respect for sources and the question of whether AI is being used as a weapon in polarisation.

06

If we had to sum it up in one piece of advice for a person who wants to use AI but does not want to lose their own voice — what would it be?

Use AI as a mirror, not as a substitute. Let it help you see options, structure, language — but the decision remains yours.

07

What, in your view, is the greatest risk when a theologian or teacher works with AI without inner discipline?

That they begin producing texts faster than they mature. AI can speed up production, but it does not speed up maturity. And if a text becomes mere output, we lose what is crucial in theology too: the ability to carry fragility.

That is why it is important to have rules: verify sources, keep one's own notes, leave time for editing and, above all, ask what the text is doing to us.

08

Can AI be a tool of synodality — a tool of listening and discernment — or is it rather a risk?

It can be both. It can help us summarise, compare, look for variants. But synodality is relational. AI does not replace human presence, empathy, responsibility.

So it is rather an aid to the process, not its core. If we use AI in preparation so that we can then listen to people better, it will serve.

09

How do we protect the authorial voice when AI can imitate a style and offer a "finished text"?

By leaving time for our own sentences — even when they are clumsy at first. The voice is born in the author's inner dialogue.

It helps to keep one's own notes from practice, to keep a journal of questions, to write short paragraphs unaided. And only then to use AI as help with structure and editing, not as the first source.

10

What workflow has proved itself for you, so that AI speeds up the work but does not steal the discernment?

Three steps. First my own outline from my head — even if it is simple. Then AI as help: structural suggestions, linguistic variants, questions I have left out.

And finally manual editing: I read, throw out foreign sentences, add experience, check sources. Thanks to that, the text remains mine — and AI remains a tool.

Theological Reflection
Key Concept

A study published in the journal Caritas et veritas on artificial intelligence and the transformation of society developed the idea of an anthropological turn towards dialogue in the age of generative AI.

The Dialogical Turn

This line is continued by Pope Leo XIV's encyclical Magnifica humanitas (2026), which places the theme of artificial intelligence within the framework of protecting the human person, dignity, truth and the common good. For this textbook, the pedagogical emphasis matters most: in a time when technologies accelerate answers, the human task remains to learn to ask good questions, to verify and to bear responsibility for the consequences.

The Vatican note Antiqua et nova (2025) points to the difference between human and artificial intelligence and stresses the need for an ethical framework that protects the dignity of the person, truth and the common good.

Theobald speaks of the "urgencies" of pastoral work — of situations that call for a new pedagogy of faith. Today this challenge includes digital literacy and ethical formation: we should use technology not as a shortcut, but as a tool that supports listening, dialogue and clearer speech. Experience with applying generative AI in pastoral theology can be a valuable laboratory in this respect, provided it remains clear that responsibility is always borne by the human person.

Transparency and the formation of the user remain important criteria.

The practical rules are simple: verify, cite, admit limits and do not let the tool decide in a person's place. When these boundaries are kept, technology can remain in the service of the relationship, not in its place.

Pope Francis, in his message for the World Day of Social Communications (2024), reminds us that technology needs a "wisdom of the heart": the ability to listen, to discern and to communicate in a way that remains human. This connects directly with the "dialogical turn": learning to lead a conversation that is not merely an exchange of information, but an encounter of persons.

The research of Bibiána Lajčinová, Jozef Žuffa and Milan Urbančok on the use of large language models in analysing religious texts shows their potential, but also the need for methodological discipline, so that the tool does not turn interpretation into quick answers without understanding.

The text on artificial intelligence as a sign of the times also addresses the theological dimension of this technology: it forces us to ask again what human dignity is, what responsibility is and how truth becomes audible in the digital space.

Key Takeaways

Necessary Skills

  • Generative AI can speed up work, but it must not speed up inaccuracy. Verification is essential.
  • Transparency about the use of AI can build trust; concealing its substantial share can weaken it.
  • An important corrective is dialogue: consultation, peer review and the ability to let oneself be corrected.
A Pause on the Journey
Personal reflection

Where does AI help me think, and where does it tempt me to skip my own maturing or responsibility?

In a group

What shared rules for using AI do we need in our academic, pastoral or professional environment?

A small step

For my next piece of work, I will separate my own outline, the AI's help, the verification of sources and the final authorial decision.

Questions for Discernment
  1. What do I use AI for — as help with thinking, or as an escape from my own responsibility?
  2. What question do I put to the technology, and what does that question reveal about my expectations?
  3. Where do I need a clear boundary about what AI should not do in my work or formation?
  4. How do I verify the facts, sources, citations and value framework of its outputs?
  5. Who is my human corrective when working with text, data or decision-making?
  6. What rules for using AI would help us learn wisely and protect human dignity?
For Groups and Facilitators
Suggested format

75-120 minutes; 8-25 people. Aim: To develop the critical, ethical and transparent use of AI while preserving human responsibility.

Opening question

Where does AI save us time, and where can it weaken verification, privacy or our own voice?

Main activity

A workflow audit: participants analyse a model AI output in terms of accuracy, sources, privacy, authorship and value framework.

Safety framework

No sensitive personal data, confidential conversations or non-anonymised research materials are entered into public AI tools.

Shared discernment

What shared rules for using AI do we need in our academic, pastoral or professional environment?

Closing step

The group creates a short checklist for the transparent and responsible use of AI.
