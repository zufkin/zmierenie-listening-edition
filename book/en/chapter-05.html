05The Fragility of Power

Reconciliation without truth and protection can be merely another pressure towards silence.

Reading approximately 20–30 minVideo 4:59 · optionalGroup optional

The full text is the core of the chapter. The video and group work are optional extensions.

In this chapter, you will learn to
  • distinguish healthy authority from control, manipulation and the abuse of power
  • name the protective role of truth, safety, transparency and shared responsibility
  • distinguish reconciliation from covering up, and forgiveness from an obligation to restore a close relationship.
Chapter videoPowerVideo in Slovak

Dialogue is not equally possible everywhere. And precisely there the fragility of power shows itself: it can be service, but also pressure; it can protect, but also wound. Sometimes dialogue stops at the threshold of fear, inequality and hurt. There the theme of power appears: how we use it, how we fear it, how it can be used to abuse trust. If we want to walk together, we need to learn the truth in this sensitive area as well.

Some conflicts in the Church are not conflicts of opinion. They are conflicts of power. Then reconciliation cannot be pursued without truth, because truth is the first form of protection. There are situations in which "being reconciled" means ceasing to normalise abuse and naming what is dangerous.

Spiritual abuse is specific in that it often happens in the name of good: in the name of obedience, purity, unity, faithfulness. That is precisely why it is hard to recognise. Especially in an environment where authority is linked with God, it easily turns into an absolute.

In the experiences of people who have passed through closed communities or manipulative relationships, a similar pattern recurs: fear, control, guilt, loss of autonomy and, finally, flight — often only when the body and the psyche can no longer cope.

This chapter does not look for culprits. It looks for a discernment: how to recognise that spirituality has turned into power, and what to do so that the wound does not spill over into further polarisation.

Dialogue

The Abuse of Spirituality

01

Can spiritual abuse be recognised by any typical signs?

Yes, although every story is different. Typically, freedom is restricted under the pretext of spiritual good: whoever disagrees with us "opposes God". Then comes control: over time, relationships, the body, information.

Another sign is manipulation through guilt: a person becomes convinced that their natural needs are sinful. Finally comes isolation, which separates them from people outside the group and weakens their chance of gaining an independent view.

02

Some communities use a system of regular "reporting". Where is the line?

Accompaniment or a regular conversation can be healthy if they protect a person's freedom and dignity. "Reporting" becomes dangerous when it serves control — when a person must report even what belongs to the sphere of intimacy, and when the information obtained is used for manipulation.

The line lies in freedom: I can say no. And in trust: the conversation must not turn into a database through which someone controls me.

03

Why do people stay in such environments even when they suffer?

Because at the beginning there is often idealism. A person wants the good, wants purity, wants to belong somewhere. And then, gradually, fear comes: if I leave, I lose everything — the community, my identity, God.

And there is shame too: "How could I have let myself be deceived like this?" Shame keeps a person in silence.

04

What is the first step when someone begins to suspect that something is not right?

The first step is to name reality without excusing it. That is hard, because abuse often teaches people to call it "love" or "obedience".

The second step is to find a safe person outside the system: someone who will not press for quick decisions, but will help them keep a clear head.

05

How can abuse be spoken about without it becoming a place of further wounding?

By separating truth from revenge. Truth is necessary so that the wound is not repeated. Revenge, however, only pours the pain onward.

In such situations, reconciliation sometimes means: securing protection, naming responsibility, giving space to healing — and leaving open whether a personal return to trust is also possible.

06

Where does the temptation of power in pastoral work come from? Why does authority turn into control so easily?

Often out of insecurity and fear. When we feel that things are falling apart, we naturally want to tighten our grip. And then power presents itself as rescue: "I will hold it together. I will set it right."

But rescue through control usually destroys the very thing it wanted to save — trust. And without trust there is no community, only a system.

07

What is the first protective element a community can have so that power does not begin to be abused?

Transparency and shared responsibility. Decisions should be explained, there should be room for questions, and important matters should not be handled "behind closed doors". Synodality is in this sense preventive: power is distributed into a responsible process and is not concentrated in a single person.

Formation is also needed. Power is not regulated by rules alone; it needs personal maturity. That means learning throughout life to work with one's own motivations, need for recognition and tiredness.

08

How do we distinguish reconciliation from covering up? Where is the line between forgiveness and denial?

Reconciliation never begins by pretending nothing happened. Such silence serves the stronger party. Forgiveness is a free possibility, not an enforced obligation. It may ripen only after the truth has been named and safety restored; in itself, it does not entail an obligation to restore a close relationship.

That is why it is important to speak also of justice, of responsibility and of protecting the weaker. This too is part of pastoral work — not as harshness, but as care for the dignity of every person.

09

What could concrete prevention of the abuse of power look like in ordinary practice — beyond the big documents?

Prevention can look simple: clear rules and intelligible channels of communication. It should be evident who decides what, how a concern can be raised and to whom a problem can be reported in confidence. Also needed is a culture in which a question is not a sign of insolence but a legitimate part of shared life.

Prevention also means that power is talked about openly. When it is not, it begins to accumulate in grey zones. And in the shadows it easily becomes a weapon.

10

What can we do when, after abuse comes to light, a community presses for "quick reconciliation" because it cannot bear the shame and the conflict?

It is important to name the difference between peace and silence. Quick reconciliation is often only a way of getting rid of tension. But the tension here carries information: something was wrong and must be clarified.

In such a situation we need to say: "We will not rush." And at the same time create concrete steps: a safe contact for reporting, professional help, clear processes. Healing needs time. And a person who has been wounded needs protection first, not pressure to forgive.

11

Once abuse has happened, what is the first sentence that can open the path towards healing without cheap reconciliation?

"I believe you. Thank you for saying it." That is a sentence which restores a person's dignity. And immediately afterwards: "We will take care of your safety."

Reconciliation without truth is only pressure towards silence. And it is precisely truth — even when it hurts — that opens the space in which, in the next chapter, we can touch what tends to be most wounded after abuse: the body and intimacy.

Theological Reflection
Key Concept

One of the key questions is the difference between authority and abused power. Healthy authority grows from competence, service and trust. The pathological use of power rests on control and fear.

Discerning Authority

In the synodal understanding of authority, power in the Church is not a possession but a commission to serve, which must be accompanied by responsibility and accountability. Authority does not rest on position alone; it earns its credibility through service and contact with people's experience. In a synodal Church, therefore, authority is not weakened when more listening happens. On the contrary, decision-making ripens in the discernment of the whole community.

In the practice of spiritual accompaniment, it is important to protect a person's freedom. A companion should not "manage" another person's life. They should create a space in which the person hears their own conscience and learns to discern before God.

That is also why one need recurs in conversations with people after an experience of abuse: to restore inner autonomy and the trust that God is not identical with the system of control.

Christian authority has its measure in the Gospel of Jesus Christ: in the way Jesus handles power — not as a stick, but as a gift for the wounded and the excluded. When authority is severed from this style, it easily turns into control. Hence also the importance of the theme of authenticity: in a polarised environment, people quickly sense a mismatch between words and stance — and trust is lost.

The synodal style shows that authority is not measured by "toughness" but by the ability to create space for the responsibility of others — without losing direction and the measure of the Gospel.

When we speak of power in the Church, we often leap quickly to the polarity "authority versus freedom". The synodal process, however, shows that authority is legitimate when it serves discernment and can bear feedback — otherwise it becomes a form of defending the system. That is also why the Synod of Bishops emphasises participation and accountability as spiritual criteria, not merely organisational measures.

The Slovak theologian Peter Ceľuch, reflecting on the formation of future priests, points out that one of the key challenges is to cultivate maturity in relation to power: learning responsibility, not privilege. Formation that does not work with the theme of power risks raising functionaries, not shepherds.

A case study from the academic environment shows that synodality begins, in practice, very "ordinarily": with the ability to listen, to name a tension without ridiculing the other side and to hold a shared framework even in conflict. In such a setting, power turns into the service of relationship.

Key Takeaways

A Return to Freedom

  • In situations of abuse, the first form of reconciliation is truth and protection. Without them, "peace" is merely the continuation of violence by other means.
  • Healthy authority is recognised by the fact that a person grows in freedom and responsibility around it, not in fear and dependence.
  • Healing after abuse is a long process. It needs a safe environment, professional help and a spiritual language that does not push a person back into guilt.
A Pause on the Journey
Personal reflection

Where in my environment do I see power that helps people grow, and where power that diminishes or silences them?

In a group

What concrete rules of transparency and feedback does our community need so that authority remains a service?

A small step

I will name one area in which responsibility, decision-making or the possibility of raising a concern needs to be explained more clearly.

Questions for Discernment
  1. Where in my environment does authority help people grow, and where does it begin to control?
  2. What stirs in me at the theme of the abuse of power — resistance, fear, anger, defensiveness, or sadness?
  3. What conviction about obedience, unity or authority do I need to examine?
  4. Who in our environment has a safe space to say "no" or to ask a question?
  5. What concrete rule of transparency would protect both the vulnerable and those in responsibility?
  6. If this topic is personally wounding, who is a safe person or professional contact outside the system?
For Groups and Facilitators
Suggested format

75-120 minutes; 6-14 people. Aim: To distinguish healthy authority, power asymmetry and the elements that protect both the vulnerable and those in responsibility.

Opening question

How do we recognise that authority in our environment truly serves?

Main activity

A map of safety and responsibility: the group distinguishes who decides, who bears the consequences, where a question can be asked and to whom a problem can be reported safely.

Safety framework

With personal experiences of abuse, no sharing of details is required. The facilitator gives priority to protecting the person over completing the programme.

Shared discernment

What concrete rules of transparency and feedback do we need so that authority remains a service?

Closing step

The group names one realistic step to strengthen transparency or safe reporting.
