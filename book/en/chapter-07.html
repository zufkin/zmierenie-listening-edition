07The Boundaries of Openness

Mature openness holds together hospitality, truthfulness and boundaries.

Reading approximately 20–30 minVideo 5:56 · optionalGroup optional

The full text is the core of the chapter. The video and group work are optional extensions.

In this chapter, you will learn to
  • distinguish openness from naivety, resignation and armouring
  • recognise your own capacity and set boundaries without aggression
  • build contact across difference through listening and appropriate trust.
Chapter videoOpennessVideo in Slovak

When we touch the body and intimacy, it becomes clear that without openness we cannot move any further. Openness, however, needs boundaries that protect the relationship, dignity and truth. It does not mean dissolving ourselves, but learning hospitality: welcoming the person while not losing the truth. It is a skill that ripens slowly.

After the experience of polarisation and hurt comes a natural reaction: closing down. Closing down protects. But if it lasts too long, it begins to isolate a person. Reconciliation, however, needs the opposite: openness. Not a naive openness, but a mature one — an openness that can hold boundaries and yet does not freeze into defence.

For many people, openness is a word that sounds dangerous. It means being vulnerable. It means admitting that not everything is under our control. Yet without this vulnerability, neither trust nor a new dialogue can arise.

Dialogue

Stepping into Risk

01

Openness sounds lovely, but why is it so demanding?

Because openness means risk. When I open up, I can be hurt. And many people have already been hurt — in the family, in the Church, at work. That is why they closed down. Closing down is sometimes a reaction to chaos.

Openness is demanding also because it changes identity. When I admit that the other may hold a piece of the truth, I have to give up the image that I alone am the "good" one.

02

How do we distinguish openness from naivety?

Openness is not the abolition of boundaries. It is rather the ability to have boundaries and yet not harden. Naivety is when a person gives trust without discernment. Openness is when I give trust gradually, in the measure of safety.

Openness can be trained: begin with a small step that is manageable.

03

What does openness do in a conversation?

Openness changes the tone. Instead of defence comes curiosity. Instead of quick judgement comes a question. And for the other person that is often a shock: suddenly they do not have to defend themselves.

Openness also makes it possible to admit a mistake. Such an admission can be a sign of mature authority, because it shows that truth matters more than one's own ego.

04

Can openness also be a spiritual category?

Yes. Openness is the way a person allows God to enter the places they wanted to protect. Not as an uninvited pressure, but as healing. Faith without openness easily turns into a system of certainties which, however, no longer truly speak to people.

Openness is also a form of hospitality: welcoming a person as a person, even when I disagree with them.

05

What would you say to someone who feels they no longer have the capacity to be open?

That this, too, is all right. Sometimes a person is exhausted and first needs to recover strength. Openness cannot be commanded. It arises where there is safety.

And safety sometimes means having at least one person beside us with whom we do not have to pretend to be someone other than who we really are. There openness is born again.

06

How can we stay open even towards what frightens us, without losing ourselves?

Openness without boundaries is naivety. Boundaries without openness are armour. What we are looking for is a different path: to be open, yet stay in contact with ourselves.

07

How do we recognise the line between openness and resignation?

By the fruit. If, after conversations, we feel more humane, even if tired, that is a good sign. If we feel cynicism, irritation and numbness building up in us, that is a signal we have gone beyond our measure.

And then it is not a failure to say: "I need a break now." That, too, is mature openness.

08

How do we keep our truthfulness in openness when we fear the other will exploit or mock us?

Openness is not an obligation to expose ourselves completely to everyone. It is the ability to share the truth in proportion to the measure of trust. Truthfulness without wisdom can cause harm.

That is why it is important to have safe relationships and safe environments. And to learn to speak the truth in a way that is neither an attack nor self-exposure. This, too, is a skill that ripens.

09

What would you advise a person who wants to be open but has the experience that openness once hurt them?

Do not rush. Openness after a wound is restored gradually. First in small things and in safe relationships.

And do not confuse healing with being "the way I was before". Perhaps I never will be. But I can be more mature: open and careful, sensitive and firm.

10

What can openness between camps look like in entirely concrete terms, so that it is not just a nice appeal?

Sometimes very little is enough: taking a step towards the person, not towards the topic. Inviting someone for coffee and asking about their story, not their opinions. Or, at a joint meeting, deliberately leaving space for one experience from the other side without comment.

Openness between camps is not primarily a debate but contact. Contact is built slowly. It is also a lifelong learning to bear the discomfort of a different perception of values and, at the same time, to understand that these values grow out of concrete stories.

11

What is the most fitting synodal step of openness a community can take when it is in tension?

Create a space in which there is no judging, only listening. It can be a meeting where each person says only what they are experiencing, not what they think about others. The others merely paraphrase their statement.

It is a simple change, yet for many communities a radical one. In it we learn that openness is a form of learning — slow and often demanding.

Theological Reflection
Key Concept

Openness can be understood as an inner hospitality: the ability to meet the other without having to defend ourselves with attacks or dissolve into naivety. It is not merely a character trait. It is a habit we learn in small steps — through conversation, through admitted uncertainty, through the courage to ask.

A Stance of Listening

Jan Loffeld points out that in an environment of religious indifference, a language built on defence stops working. If God is "missed by no one", openness becomes the basic precondition for the question of meaning and of God to arise at all.

In practice, openness also means a culture of encounter: creating spaces where people do not have to sort themselves into camps immediately. That is also why it matters that the theme of openness sounds at the academic, formational and public levels within the life of the theological faculty.

Openness is therefore more than a strategy: it is a stance of service and listening. Tomáš Halík adds that the maturing of faith means learning to converse with one's own doubts as well — and that is the foundation of genuine openness.

This diagnosis helps us understand why many pastoral responses miss their mark: people often do not reject God, but lack the experience that faith could be relevant to their everyday life. Halík also shows that Christianity is moving into its "afternoon" — a time when repeating formulas is no longer enough, and a more patient faith able to bear questions must ripen instead.

Openness is therefore neither naive tolerance nor a relativising of doctrine. It is the ability to lead a conversation in such a way that the truth can be heard in a concrete life — and that the person does not feel humiliated in advance. This openness can be learnt as a skill: asking questions without irony, naming boundaries without aggression and distinguishing between a person and their opinion. Such a culture of conversation is one of the forms in which the Church, in the "afternoon" of Christianity, can remain a home for seekers as well.

Openness likewise learns patience: not everything can be resolved quickly, and not every question has an immediate answer. Halík reminds us that the maturing of faith often passes through a period of "half-light" in which it matters more to remain faithful and to listen than to be the winner.

A theology of openness recalls that, in pastoral work, openness is often confused with boundlessness. In reality it is first of all a stance of listening: the ability to accept that the other person is more than their opinion, while not renouncing the truth. Boundaries are then not drawn against the person, but protect the relationship and dignity.

Theobald describes the "style" of the Church as a fraternity that shows itself in the manner of communication. Where openness is built on humility and on the recognition of dignity, it ceases to be a threat to identity and becomes a place of mission.

Halík adds that the courage to change is often born precisely in the encounter with otherness. Openness is therefore not a retreat from faith, but the way faith learns to speak in new situations without aggression.

Key Takeaways

A Constant Movement

  • Openness is not a one-off decision. It is a gradual movement from defence to trust.
  • Healthy openness has boundaries. Without them it can lead to exhaustion.
  • Openness is most convincing in concrete conversations, when a person listens and does not humiliate.
A Pause on the Journey
Personal reflection

Where do I need to take a small step of openness, and where, on the contrary, do I need to protect my own boundary more clearly?

In a group

How can our community build contact with people who are different, without demanding immediate agreement from them?

A small step

I will choose one person or group I tend to perceive through a label, and ask one question about their concrete experience.

Questions for Discernment
  1. Where have I recently opened up, and where have I rather closed down?
  2. What awakens openness in me towards a different person or opinion?
  3. What boundaries do I need so that my openness is neither naivety nor defence?
  4. Which label do I use instead of a precise naming of the problem?
  5. With whom can I hold a safe conversation across our differences without losing my own identity?
  6. What small step of openness is realistic and responsible for me today?
For Groups and Facilitators
Suggested format

60-90 minutes; 6-18 people. Aim: To distinguish between hospitality, a healthy boundary, escape and aggressive closing down.

Opening question

Where in our environment is openness confused with boundlessness, and a boundary with the rejection of a person?

Main activity

The continuum of openness: participants work with model situations and distinguish between hospitality, a healthy boundary, escape and aggressive closing down.

Safety framework

Openness must not be confused with an obligation to remain in a harmful or abusive relationship.

Shared discernment

How can we build contact with people who are different, without demanding immediate agreement from them?

Closing step

The group names one small step of contact and one boundary that will make this contact safer.
