01Signs of the Times

Reconciliation begins when we stop overlooking reality.

In this chapter, you will learn to
  • perceive the signs of the times as stories and changes in people's lives
  • distinguish between the noisy news of the day and the deeper kairos
  • create a space in which questions can be born again.
Chapter videoSignsVideo in Slovak

In the introduction we attuned ourselves to a journey that is not about winning an argument but about attentiveness. The first step is simple and demanding at once: learning to see reality as it is. The signs of the times are not a riddle for forecasters. They are a question that life puts to each of us.

Nor are the signs of the times a list of problems to be managed in our communications. They are signals that the ground is shifting beneath our feet — in society, in the Church and in personal stories. Anyone who wants to speak about reconciliation first needs to see what is actually happening around us, and to sense why the old answers have stopped being intelligible or effective.

One experience recurs in many debates about religion: people no longer argue about God. Rather, they have stopped asking whether God is needed at all. This shift is decisive. It changes the language in which faith is spoken about, it changes trust in authorities, and it changes the way hope is born.

Reading the signs of the times is therefore the first step of reconciliation. Not because we should immediately name the culprits of unwelcome changes, but because without a truthful naming of reality, spiritual words turn into a defence mechanism. And while defence may protect an identity, it does not heal relationships.

Pastoral theology today faces a simple yet demanding question: how do we create a space in which questions can be born again — in an environment that no longer waits for ready-made answers?

Dialogue

How to Read a World That Has Gone Quiet

01

When you say "signs of the times", what do you mean? Is it more of a diagnosis, or spiritual discernment?

It is a way of paying attention rather than a diagnosis. A diagnosis tends to close things into categories — and then all we do is look for a solution. The signs of the times remain open. They do not say, "This is the problem." They ask instead: "What is happening here? What is changing? What is ripening? What is dying away?"

Discernment is a process. Sometimes it is a patient naming of reality. Sometimes it means admitting that we do not yet understand. And sometimes it is the courage to let reality correct us.

02

Many people imagine a list of topics: secularisation, the crisis of the Church, polarisation… Is that what it is?

Those topics are visible, but if we stop at them, we miss the core. The signs of the times are not an overview of bullet points for a presentation. They are stories that happen to people. And it is precisely through stories that we can grasp why someone has gone silent, why they have withdrawn, why they have lost trust.

If theology holds on to concepts alone, it may be right — and still miss the person. The signs of the times teach us to look where meaning is breaking down.

03

How do we avoid simply projecting our own fears or wishes onto reality?

Two things help: staying in contact with people, and learning to listen without immediately giving advice. The second thing is awareness of our own filters. Each of us has a "map of the world", and we often do not even notice that we filter everything through it.

That is why discernment must not be a solo performance. It needs community, conversation, correction. Mediation teaches us the same: first check whether we have understood correctly, and only then interpret.

04

What is the strongest sign of the times for you today?

One of the strongest is silence. The silence that follows once the old language has stopped working. Many people carry an experience of being hurt, of weariness with moralising, or of disappointment with authorities. Then came apathy. And apathy is serious: it says that things are "no longer worth the energy".

If someone is still fighting, they still care. When someone simply leaves, it often means they have stopped feeling seen.

05

And where is God in all this? If people are silent, is that not a sign of God's absence?

It may also be a sign that God is no longer associated with a language people can hear. Faith in the God of the incarnation, however, says that God enters concrete human situations — including silence, disappointment, and the search for dignity.

Sometimes the first place of encounter is not an answer but a safe space: someone listens to me and I do not have to defend myself. That is a deeply theological experience.

06

You speak of space. What does that mean in practice?

In practice it means creating a conversation in which a person does not have to fear being judged straight away. It means asking differently: not "why don't you believe?", but "where did your trust break down?", "what is missing for you?", "what did you learn about God that no longer works for you?"

It also means admitting that some of our answers were quick, but not healing. And that reconciliation sometimes begins with an apology for the way we have spoken.

07

How does this touch the work of a theologian, a mediator or a counsellor?

It touches the basic stance. A theologian without contact with reality risks speaking precisely — and beside the point. A mediator without sensitivity to the "signs of the times" risks resolving only surface disputes while overlooking deep wounds. And a counsellor without discernment can easily mistake a spiritual problem for a psychological one, or the other way round.

The signs of the times teach us to read what lies behind the words. And that is the foundation of reconciliation.

08

What is the first step when we sense that "the world has gone quiet"?

The first step is to stop hardening our arguments. Sometimes the Church responds to silence by sharpening its language or adding more rules. But silence born of exhaustion only deepens as a result.

The first step is a return to human speech: to story, to question, to listening. Only then can we look for where, within that story, a space for God might open up again.

09

When does discernment become mere "analysis" without the courage to act?

When we use discernment as protection against our own risk. It is easier to name a situation than to enter a relationship. And reconciliation is always a risk: I may be misunderstood, rejected, I may touch pain.

That is why discernment needs a final step: a concrete act. Sometimes it is a conversation. Sometimes it is a change of style. At other times it is creating a space where people can meet without fear.

10

To simplify: what is the first step when we want to learn to read the signs of the times without panic?

Start with what is closest to us — our own experience. Notice what stirs fear in us, what awakens hope, where our breathing quickens and where our heart starts pounding. The signs of the times are often reflected in the body, not in the head.

And then go to people. Ask, listen, do not rush to judge. Not so that we can have a quick interpretation, but so that we stay in contact with reality.

11

Is there a difference between a "sign of the times" and what is merely the noisy news of the day? How do we avoid mistaking one for the other?

The news of the day often provokes us to a quick reaction. The signs of the times lead us to a deeper movement: to the question of what is changing in a person, in relationships, in the way people search for meaning.

It helps to ask what is merely the emotion of the moment and what leaves a lasting trace; and also what brings more life. Not more noise, but more humanity.

12

Looking at a concrete parish or community: where should we look for the signs of the times there?

Often precisely in what has changed quietly. Who has stopped coming, and why. What questions people bring to funerals, baptisms, confessions, and conversations after Mass. And also where a new sensitivity is emerging: to dignity, to justice, to mental health, for example.

The signs of the times are not only big themes. They are small shifts in what people can bear, what they believe, what they fear. And when we catch them, we can speak more precisely — not more loudly.

13

How do we recognise that we have merely projected a "sign" out of our own fears or our own camp?

One simple check helps: what fruit does our interpretation bear? If it grows into closedness, a need for an enemy, or a feeling of superiority, we have probably just bent reality to fit us. If it grows into compassion, a willingness to learn, and greater patience, we are closer to discernment.

And then there is the synodal rule: do not stay alone with it. Discernment happens in conversation — with people we trust, and with those who see things differently. When we can hold that, we suddenly find that this is not a diagnosis of the world, but a time that asks something of us.

Theological Reflection
Key Concept

Pastoral theology increasingly works with the distinction between "time that passes" and "time that speaks to us". Chronos is measurable time: deadlines, the calendar, the plan. Kairos is qualitative time: the moment in which a turning point, a challenge or a new ripening becomes visible. Reading the signs of the times means perceiving the kairos — and not letting it slip away.

Kairos: A Time That Asks Something of Us

The French theologian Christoph Theobald points out that a theology of the present is not merely an updating of old themes. It is a theology of conversion: the ability to let ourselves be turned around by a reality that disturbs us, without losing the core of the Gospel.

In this spirit, Theobald describes pastoral theology as a reading of faith within "events" in which the way the Gospel can be heard is changing. Kairos, in this sense, is recognised not by its noise but by the fact that it forces us to slow down and to name what is breaking or ripening within us and between us. It is not a slogan but a moment of truthfulness: what a particular time asks of us, so that we do not remain at the level of reflex but also take a small step towards reconciliation.

Jan Loffeld names one of the most difficult transformations: religious indifference. Not aggressive atheism, but a state in which God is "missed by no one". This means that reconciliation often begins not with a defence of the faith, but with a renewal of the question of meaning, trust and hope.

This quiet disengagement places demands on both language and method: instead of presupposing faith, we learn to listen first. Precisely here, working with generative AI can also make sense — as a tool for sorting themes, questions and linguistic possibilities — provided it remains under the control of discernment and personal responsibility.

That is why this book takes the reading of reality seriously. If we do not face silence and apathy truthfully, we will only keep repeating our own answers. But if we learn to read the kairos, even silence can become a beginning — not an end.

Kairos is thus also a test of our ability to learn: to accept that some pastoral models no longer work, while not losing confidence that the Gospel still has something to say. Theobald's "pastoral conversion" and Loffeld's diagnosis of disengagement meet here in one challenge: to seek a language and gestures that are truthful and, at the same time, intelligible in today's world.

In practice this means a shift from "answers prepared in advance" to working with questions: what hurts people today, what they long for, what they fear. Kairos often shows itself in a changing vocabulary — a credible witness and the ability to stay with a person achieve more than arguments do.

The Czech priest and theologian Tomáš Halík reminds us that Christianity in Europe is entering its "afternoon": a period in which the self-evident religious mechanisms no longer function, and faith is therefore learning to speak the language of witness rather than the language of pressure. That, too, is kairos: a moment when reality pushes us towards maturing, not towards barricading ourselves behind past certainties.

In the Slovak context, a similar wisdom of discernment appears in local pastoral-theological reflections: wisdom in a time of uncertainty is not a quick answer, but the ability to stay with the question, with the person and with the word of God long enough for what is essential to emerge from what is merely the noise of the day.

Key Takeaways

The Sign as an Invitation

  • The signs of the times do not teach us what to think. They teach us how to look.
  • Reconciliation does not begin with big words. It begins with a small change of posture: ceasing to react automatically, listening longer and speaking more slowly.
  • If people are silent today, it is often not because they have no questions. Rather, they fear receiving an answer without a relationship. The practice of reconciliation therefore begins with building a space in which a question can be voiced at all.
A Pause on the Journey
Personal reflection

Which silence or change around me do I need to stop explaining and start listening to more attentively?

In a group

Whose voice is missing in our community when we name what is changing today?

A small step

This week I will ask one person an open question without offering an immediate answer.

Questions for Discernment
  1. Which features of today's society stir fear in me, and which stir curiosity?
  2. Where do I encounter the fact that people no longer hear the old answers?
  3. What silence or absent question do I sense in my family, my work, my parish or my wider social environment?
  4. Whose voice do we tend to overlook when reading the "signs of the times"?
  5. Which Gospel criterion helps me distinguish between defending an identity and being open to reality?
  6. What small step this week could create more safety for a conversation about what is happening?
For Groups and Facilitators
Suggested format

60-90 minutes; 6-16 people. Aim: To develop attentiveness to quiet changes, missing voices and questions that do not yet have a ready-made answer.

Opening question

Which change in our environment do we notice but not yet understand?

Main activity

A map of quiet changes: the group names who has stopped coming, what questions are emerging and what has changed without public discussion.

Safety framework

We speak in the first person, no one is pressed to share personal stories, and we distinguish experience from judgement.

Shared discernment

Whom do we need to invite or hear so that our discernment is not merely the voice of the majority?

Closing step

Each person names one voice or question they want to attend to during the coming week.
