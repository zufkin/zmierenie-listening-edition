ACTWALK IN HOPE

12Pilgrims of Hope

Hope is a practice of small steps that keep relationship, trust and meaning alive.

Reading approximately 20–30 minVideo 2:29 · optionalGroup optional

The full text is the core of the chapter. The video and group work are optional extensions.

In this chapter, you will learn to
  • distinguish hope from optimism and from waiting for guarantees
  • join reconciliation with truth, safety, boundaries and perseverance
  • choose a realistic next step in a concrete relationship or community.
Chapter videoHopeVideo in Slovak

In this final chapter we return to the horizon without which the journey becomes mere survival. Hope is not a mood or optimism. It is a stance we learn in reality and in community. The Jubilee Year 2025 reminded us that we are pilgrims — people who walk together.

Reconciliation is not a state in which nothing hurts any more. It is a way in which a person carries pain so that further hatred does not grow out of it. That is why reconciliation is always joined to hope — not to naivety, but to the decision to keep walking.

The Jubilee Year carried the motto "Pilgrims of Hope". Pilgrimage is a good image: on a pilgrimage, one does not walk because the road is comfortable. One walks because the direction makes sense. And hope is not a feeling. Hope is a practice: concrete steps that keep a person alive.

This last chapter joins the preceding themes into a single journey: seeing reality, discerning it in truth, and acting so that space for trust can arise. Not ideally, but really.

FROM THE ORIGINAL CONVERSATION · OPTIONAL

Pilgrims of Hope: Reconciliation as a Journey

A short personal reflection on hope, pilgrimage, surrender and the experience of journeying. The video closes the series with an image of reconciliation as a process in which a person continues walking without the guarantee of a simple outcome.

Dialogue

Deliberate Small Steps

01

What is the difference between optimism and hope?

Optimism expects that everything will turn out well. Hope is deeper: even when things do not turn out as we imagined, doing good still makes sense. Hope leans on faithfulness, not on guarantees.

In a crisis, optimism can run dry. Hope, however, can remain even when our expectations are not fulfilled.

02

How is hope learnt in practice?

It is learnt in small steps: renewing a conversation that broke off; admitting a mistake; creating a safe place for any question. Hope is not a grand project. It is everyday faithfulness.

Sometimes hope is learnt by persevering in therapy, counselling or spiritual accompaniment. That, too, is hope: not letting oneself be carried off by a shortcut.

03

How do we work with the fact that not everyone wants reconciliation?

Reconciliation cannot be forced. It can be offered. Sometimes the other is not yet ready. Then it is important to protect boundaries and, at the same time, not to harden.

Hope is also the ability to wait without resignation.

04

What, in your view, is the greatest test of reconciliation in the Church today?

Perhaps the ability to admit wounds while remaining authentic. And the ability to lead a dialogue without the truth turning into a weapon. These are tests that cannot be managed with learned methods. They need personal maturity.

And that is born where truth, humility and practice come together.

05

If you had to name one image that holds the whole book together, what would it be?

Pilgrimage. A pilgrimage on which a person carries their own wound, the wound of the Church and the wound of society — and learns not to make a weapon of it. They learn to make a path of it.

Reconciliation, in this sense, is a spiritual literacy: knowing how to read, to discern and to act.

06

What does "hope" mean in a time of exhaustion? How does it become a path and not a slogan?

Hope is not a feeling we manufacture. It is a direction we choose. In a time of exhaustion, hope is often very small: that today we will take one step, that today we will not break a relationship, that today we will not betray our own conscience.

When hope becomes a path, it ceases to be motivation and becomes practice. It is faithfulness in small things: the conversation we do not put off, the apology we voice, the silence we allow ourselves.

07

How do we protect our hope when things do not change quickly — in the Church, in society, in our own stories?

By learning to work on ourselves. Not so that we change others, but so that we truthfully accept the reality we cannot change straight away. Hope is also the ability to stay where we are without resignation.

And community helps. Not grand declarations, but people with whom we can be truthful. That is synodal walking: carrying one another.

08

What does the Jubilee Year symbolically remind us of when we speak of reconciliation?

That there is a time when things return to their place. When debts are forgiven, when pressure eases, when a person does not have to carry the same burden all their life.

The Jubilee is not a magic shortcut. It builds on the biblical tradition of renewal, the forgiveness of debts and a new beginning. Therein lies its pastoral wisdom: reconciliation can also mean a new beginning we allow ourselves to accept.

09

Where, quite concretely, does reconciliation begin in everyday life today?

It often begins in small things: in being able to see a fellow pilgrim in the other person, and in setting boundaries that grow out of self-acceptance.

And also in re-creating a rhythm of learning: what did not work for us in the conflict, what we would do differently next time. For reconciliation is also memory — not the memory of grievance, but the memory of learning.

10

What do we do when we attempt reconciliation and fail? When we feel it cannot be done?

Failure in reconciliation does not mean we are condemned to division. It means we have hit a limit of capacity — our own or the other's.

Sometimes the step of hope is precisely to give ourselves time, to return to ourselves and to learn from what happened. Then we can try again — perhaps in a different way, perhaps with the help of a third person, wherever a further attempt is safe and responsible. Hope lies not in always succeeding, but in not giving up the possibility of learning and doing good.

11

If we were to invite the reader to one concrete step after finishing this book, what would it be?

To take a small step towards reconciliation where it is closest to us. Not straight away in the hardest conflict. Perhaps naming that we are tired. Perhaps ceasing to say "they" and beginning to say "I".

And then letting it ripen. Reconciliation is not a one-off feat. It is a journey on which we learn to see, to discern and to act so that we can walk together. That is why the book can close with a short conclusion and continue with appendices offering practical tools.

Theological Reflection
Key Concept

The programme "Pilgrims of Hope on the Journey of Reconciliation" at the University of the Third Age shows that reconciliation can be learnt as a journey: through discerning the signs of the times, through dialogue and through the practical skills of mediation and accompaniment.

Still on the Move

The Jubilee framework reminds us that hope is not only a private emotion. It is also a social virtue: creating conditions in which people do not have to live in fear, isolation and hopelessness.

The conclusion of this book is therefore simple: reconciliation does not begin in ideal conditions. It begins where a person takes the first step — even without knowing how it will turn out. Just like Abraham: he sets out because he believes the journey can be a blessing.

Tomáš Halík joins hope with the patient courage to stay on the road even when the shape of faith is changing. Part of this is the need for discernment: not everything new is good, but neither is everything old automatically safe. Christoph Theobald's pastoral thought helps us hold on to reality: hope is born in concrete relationships, not in abstract rhetoric.

Pope Francis's bull Spes non confundit (2024) reminds us that hope is a gift, but also a task: it has a social dimension and must show itself in concrete steps of solidarity. That is why the practical rhythm of "see - discern - act" is more than a methodology; it is the way hope is learnt: first naming reality truthfully, then confronting it with the Gospel, and finally taking a step that is small but real.

Miroslav Volf points out that reconciliation also requires boundaries: "embrace" is not a denial of the truth, but a decision not to live by exclusion. Hope as shalom means wholeness — not because everything will be resolved, but because we learn to be together without humiliation and fear.

The image of pilgrimage is apt: hope is lived in movement and in community, not in isolation. When we walk together, we learn more easily to see, to discern and to act — and even the "embrace" takes concrete shape in everyday gestures.

The synodal process reminds us that hope is not an emotion, but a way of walking: a community learns to carry differences, to discern and not to lose direction. The Final Document of the Synod of Bishops (2024) therefore emphasises communio and missio: hope becomes visible where the Church learns participation and where responsibility is shared.

The research of Michal Vašečka and Jozef Žuffa on the crisis of elites shows that mistrust in society is not merely a political issue. It is also a spiritual sign: it raises the question of whether we still believe that truth and justice are possible. That is also why hope in pastoral work is joined to the renewal of trust — in small steps, in community and in the meaning of service.

Christoph Theobald speaks of the "style" of Christianity as fraternity: hope endures when it becomes a way of life — and not merely a slogan. Pilgrims of hope are those who learn fraternity even in conflict.

Key Takeaways

A Way of Life

  • Hope is not a feeling but a practice: small steps that keep a relationship alive.
  • Reconciliation joins truth and safety. Without truth it is cheap. Without safety it can be violent.
  • One of theology's important services today is human speech: a speech that carries mystery, but does not wound.
A Pause on the Journey
Personal reflection

What small step of reconciliation is realistic, safe and truthful in my life today?

In a group

Where does our community need to cultivate hope as a shared practice, not merely as an encouraging word?

A small step

I will name one concrete step, the person responsible for carrying it out and the date on which we will return to it.

Questions for Discernment
  1. Where do I need to take the first small step of reconciliation today?
  2. What strengthens hope in me, and what weakens it?
  3. Where do I tend to resign, and how do I recognise it in myself in time?
  4. Who is my fellow pilgrim who helps me walk truthfully and humanely?
  5. What concrete act in the coming month could reduce polarisation in my environment?
  6. Where do we need to cultivate hope as a practice, not as a slogan?
For Groups and Facilitators
Suggested format

75-105 minutes; 6-20 people. Aim: To translate hope into a realistic, safe and shared next step.

Opening question

Where in our environment does hope become a slogan, and where does it turn into concrete practice?

Main activity

A map of the next step: the group names the reality, the sources of hope, the boundaries and one achievable act for the next 30 days.

Safety framework

Hope must not be used to deny pain or to press for quick reconciliation. The step must be safe and responsible.

Shared discernment

Where do we need to cultivate hope as a shared practice, not merely as an encouraging word?

Closing step

The group determines one concrete step, the person responsible and the date of a shared return to evaluate it.
