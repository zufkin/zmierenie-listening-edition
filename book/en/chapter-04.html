04Dialogue and Nonviolent Communication

Dialogue is a way of remaining truthful without humiliating the other, and of building a shared space even without full agreement.

Reading approximately 20–30 minVideo 5:45 · optionalGroup optional

The full text is the core of the chapter. The video and group work are optional extensions.

In this chapter, you will learn to
  • distinguish dialogue from dispute, retreat and manipulation
  • create a safe framework through listening, paraphrase, open questions and clear rules
  • recognise when a conversation needs a boundary, a break or the help of a third person.

This chapter therefore presents dialogue as a space "in between" — between conviction and the concrete story, between the truth and the way we voice it, between protecting our own boundaries and the willingness to understand the other.

At the Faculty of Theology of Trnava University, dialogue is cultivated in interfaith encounters, in the study of mediation, in teaching and in public discussions. What proves decisive is not whether people eventually agree, but whether they can listen to one another and stay in contact without ridicule or humiliation.

The word dialogue is used very often today, yet it can denote different realities — from a polite exchange of views to an encounter that changes the way a person understands both themselves and the other. Reconciliation needs precisely the second kind of dialogue: not merely a communication technique, but a stance of openness, truthfulness and respect.

Dialogue is not weakness, not retreat, and not a more polite form of dispute. It is a way of returning to our common human ground and of carrying difference without the relationship having to fall apart. In this sense it is also a community-building practice: it makes it possible to stay together and continue in shared discernment even where we do not fully agree.

Having learnt to see the signs of the times, to slow down and to recognise the mechanisms of polarisation, the natural question arises whether we are still able to stay in conversation. It is not enough to name that something hurts. We need to create a space in which one can speak truthfully without humiliating the other.

Dialogue, Listening and Boundaries

What does it mean to listen to a person with whom we fundamentally disagree? A conversation about dialogue, the risks of openness, the need for clear boundaries, and ways of defending what matters without humiliating the other person.

Dialogue

Even When We Disagree

01

What is dialogue, in your view? Isn't it just a polite form of dispute?

Dialogue is the way a person learns to be truthful without destroying the other. In a dispute, the point is often victory. In dialogue, the point is understanding — and understanding is not agreement.

Dialogue is also a risk: it admits that the other may have something to teach me. And that is unpleasant for the ego.

02

When is dialogue impossible?

When the other side is unwilling to recognise the dignity of their partner. Or when the conversation is merely a cover for manipulation. Then boundaries need to be protected.

Dialogue is also impossible when a person cannot bear their own emotion. If there is too much anger or fear in us, the conversation turns into an explosion. Then the first step is slowing down and stabilising.

03

How can a safe space for dialogue be created?

A safe space does not arise from rules alone. It arises from the tone of voice, the ability to listen, the willingness to admit one's own limits. A clear framework also helps: what the goal is, what the boundary is, and what is out of bounds (humiliation, irony, labels).

In mediation, one simple agreement has proved its worth as a starting point: we will speak in the first person and try to describe our own experience, not judge the other.

04

Many people want to "win with an argument" straight away. What about that?

An argument has its place, but only after listening. If I use an argument as the first thing, I often cancel the relationship by doing so. The other person then stops listening and begins to defend themselves.

Dialogue also needs questions that are not traps but open up space: "What matters most to you in this?" or "How does this touch your life?"

05

What is the biggest mistake in dialogue?

When a person enters a conversation with the feeling that they already know everything, the dialogue turns into a lecture. And a lecture does not reconcile.

The second mistake is tiredness: when a person no longer has the capacity to listen. Then it is better to postpone the conversation than to destroy it.

06

How does dialogue relate to faith?

Faith in a God who enters history is itself "dialogical". God does not communicate only through commands, but also through story, questions and journey. The Bible is built on stories in which dialogue is renewed between people, and between the human person and God.

If relationship stands at the heart of faith, then our communication should be relational too. Dialogue is therefore not only a method, but a spiritual stance.

07

If we were to describe dialogue in entirely practical terms: what is the first step that decides whether a conversation opens up or closes down?

The first step is tone and intention. Whether we come to persuade or to understand. People sense it within the first minute. And once they sense pressure, they begin to defend themselves — even if we were speaking in beautiful sentences.

Dialogue begins where we allow ourselves to say: "Help me understand this."

08

Can dialogue be learnt? How do we train it in a community so that it is not just theory?

It can. Dialogue is a skill. It is trained in small things: in not interrupting, in asking follow-up questions, in learning to paraphrase. One simple exercise helps greatly: one person speaks without interruption, the other only summarises what they have heard — without judging.

And one more thing: allowing ourselves silence. Sometimes the greatest gift of dialogue is that we do not have to answer each other straight away.

09

How do we discern when dialogue is still possible and when boundaries need to be set?

Dialogue is possible wherever at least a minimal willingness to listen exists. It does not require immediate agreement, but a basic readiness to accept that the other person has the right to speak. When a conversation turns into repeated humiliation, a boundary is legitimate.

A boundary is not the end of dialogue. It is the protection of dignity. Sometimes it is the only way a conversation can one day be resumed without further wounding.

10

How do we work with the fact that under the word "dialogue" people often imagine weakness or retreat?

It has to be translated into experience. Dialogue is not retreat. It is the courage to come closer. The courage to hear what the other is really saying, even when I disagree with it.

And it helps to name that dialogue protects the truth. Because truth without relationship turns into ideology. Dialogue is a way of carrying the truth so that it does not humiliate a person.

11

What do we do when dialogue fails and we feel like giving up?

Sometimes it is more honest to take a break than to continue the fight. To say: "I cannot go on right now. I need time." That, too, is dialogue, if it is said with respect.

And if the situation keeps repeating itself, it is legitimate to invite a third person — a mediator, a facilitator, someone who helps hold the framework. Because dialogue is not only spontaneity. It is also the protection of the relationship. And it is precisely the protection of relationship that leads us, in the next chapter, to the theme of power.

Theological Reflection
Key Concept

Dialogue is a safe and truthful space in which the other person does not have to stop being different in order to remain part of the relationship and the community.

Dialogue as a Spiritual Stance

Dialogue is not merely a transfer of information. It is a space in which a person gradually comes to recognise what is going on within them. That is why it needs a safe framework, time and accompaniment free of manipulation. Christian faith has a dialogical structure. God enters human history, addresses the person, asks questions and leads them along a path. If relationship stands at the heart of faith, the manner of communication should likewise protect relationship and human dignity.

Truth as a Shared Space

Dialogue does not mean giving up on truth. It protects truth from becoming an instrument of power. It does not begin with the question "Who is right?", but with the question: How can truth be sought and voiced without destroying either the person or the shared space? In this sense, dialogue is an ethos — a way in which a person remains faithful to their conviction while recognising the dignity of the other.

Dialogue as a Community-Building Practice

Christoph Theobald reminds us that not all tensions can be resolved by pressure or an immediate decision. Some must be carried in patience and in a process of shared discernment. Carlo Maria Martini understood dialogue with non-believers not as a pastoral tactic, but as a way for the Church to stay in contact with the questions of its age. Systematic training in listening, paraphrase, mediation and Nonviolent Communication is then not merely a technical aid. It is the formation of a community capable of bearing difference without excluding a person. The publication Science, Society and Religion in Dialogue broadens this perspective to dialogue between different ways of knowing. Difference need not be an obstacle to shared discernment; it can be its source.

Key Takeaways

A Safe Space

  • Dialogue does not arise merely because people meet. It needs a framework of dignity, safety and clear rules.
  • First listening, then checking that we have understood, and only then an argument. This order protects both the relationship and the truth.
  • Dialogue need not lead to full agreement. It can, however, enable people to stay together and carry difference without humiliation.
  • If a conversation repeatedly wounds or manipulates, setting a boundary is not a failure of dialogue but a protection of dignity.
A Pause on the Journey
Personal reflection

In which conversation today do I need to understand more than to persuade? What can I do so that the other person does not have to defend themselves against me?

In a group

Which rule would most help our community stay in conversation even in disagreement? How can we concretely introduce it into our meetings?

A small step

In the next difficult conversation, I will first summarise in my own words what I have heard, and only then voice my position.

Questions for Discernment
  1. In which conversations do I begin to argue rather than listen?
  2. What happens in me when someone disagrees with what I consider important?
  3. What sentence of disagreement can I voice without ridicule or humiliation?
  4. Which needs of the other side do I perhaps hear too late?
  5. What framework of safety do we need so that a conversation is not a battle for victory?
  6. Which question should our community learn to ask more often?
For Groups and Facilitators
Suggested format

60-90 minutes; 6-16 people. Aim: To practise listening, paraphrase and voicing disagreement without humiliation.

Opening question

What most prevents me from listening in a difficult conversation?

Main activity

Listening without defence: in pairs, one person describes for three minutes a situation in which they felt unheard; the other, without advice or argument, summarises what they have heard. The speaker confirms or refines the understanding, then the roles are swapped. The group names what opened the conversation up and what closed it down.

Safety framework

We speak in the first person, we do not disclose other people's stories without consent, everyone may decline to answer, we do not use irony or labels, and if strong emotions arise we slow the conversation down or pause it. The facilitator does not push the group towards agreement or towards reconciliation at any cost.

Shared discernment

What rule of communication does our group need so that difference does not lead to broken relationships?

Closing step

Each person names one communication skill they want to practise consciously during the coming week.
