06The Vulnerability of Identity

The body, intimacy and identity need a language that holds together truthfulness, dignity and safety.

Reading approximately 20–30 minVideo 4:49 · optionalGroup optional

The full text is the core of the chapter. The video and group work are optional extensions.

In this chapter, you will learn to
  • distinguish between healthy moral responsibility, guilt and wounding shame
  • speak about the body and intimacy precisely, humanely and without humiliation
  • create a safe framework that protects conscience, dignity and boundaries.
Chapter videoIdentityVideo in Slovak

The theme of power naturally leads us to the vulnerability of identity. And nowhere are we more vulnerable than in the area of the body and intimacy. Here the longing for closeness meets the longing for control. If we want to understand, we need gentleness, truthfulness and the ability to discern without humiliating.

The body is a place where reconciliation often begins. Many spiritual conflicts are not only "in the head". They show themselves in the body: in tension, shame, fear of one's own desire, or the inability to accept one's own vulnerability.

In some forms of Christian spirituality, the body was long understood as an obstacle and sexuality as a danger. The result was often a double morality: control on the outside, chaos within. Questions of intimacy therefore belong among the most sensitive topics in the church environment as well.

This chapter does not enter into the details of moral disputes. It focuses on a more basic step: how to speak about the body and intimacy truthfully, humanely and healingly, and how to accompany people who, in the area of sexuality, find themselves caught in a conflict between faith, the body and their own identity.

Dialogue

On Intimacy without Shame

01

Why is the topic of sexuality in the Church so charged?

Because it touches deep identity. Sexuality is not just behaviour. It is the energy of relationship, desire, closeness. If a person experiences their desire being treated as "a problem", they easily begin to believe that the problem is themselves.

And when a moralising language is added, shame arises. Shame is one of the hardest emotions, because it isolates a person.

02

How can healthy morality be distinguished from shame?

Morality in the healthy sense protects the good: dignity, faithfulness and safety. Shame, however, does not protect the good; it can destroy a person. If, after "moral guidance", a person feels only misunderstood, rejected or despairing, we must ask whether the manner of accompaniment was appropriate.

A good moral framework leads to responsibility, not to self-hatred.

03

How does the LGBT topic enter into this?

In practice it turns out that many LGBT people do not live in conflict with God, but in conflict with the language the Church uses about them. They have often experienced rejection, irony or silence. And then they no longer perceive that there could be space in the Church for their story.

Pastoral care therefore does not begin with a debate about morality. It begins with recognising dignity and with a safe conversation.

04

What is the biggest mistake when accompanying a person in the area of sexuality?

Mistaking the person for a problem. When a person becomes "a topic", we lose contact. And without contact, all that remains is lecturing.

Another mistake is ignoring the body. If a person speaks of anxiety, of loneliness, of the need for closeness, and we answer only with a norm, they will feel unheard and unaccepted.

05

Can chastity be spoken of in a way that heals?

Yes, if we understand chastity not as control but as integrity. Integrity means that my body, emotions and values are in harmony. It is not sterility, but truthfulness.

Chastity is then not "being unbodily". It is being responsible in how I handle the body and desire — so that I do not harm myself or others.

06

What would you say to a person who feels their body is an enemy?

That the body is rather a place where truth can be met. The body is honest. It shows stress, tiredness, sadness, desire. And when a person learns to listen to the body, they also begin to learn humility.

Reconciliation with the body often means ceasing to punish it and beginning to perceive it as a gift — even when it is wounded.

07

Why is the topic of the body so sensitive? Why do shame or fighting flare up so quickly around it?

Because the body carries memory. It remembers touches that were safe and those that were not. It remembers where we were accepted and where we were shamed. And when a religious language that has judged the body for years is added, an explosive mixture arises.

The body is also a place of identity. When we speak about the body, we speak about who I am. That is why this topic so easily turns into ideology — because it is the defence of a wounded place.

08

What do we need in order to be able to speak of the body and intimacy with respect — in the Church and in society alike?

First safety, not theory. Safety means that a person can name even what they themselves are not yet clear about, and their statement will not be used against them. Then a language rooted in the first person is needed: "This is how I experience it," instead of a general verdict: "This is how it should be."

Asking about experience helps greatly: "What is going on within you? Where do you feel the tension? What do you need in order to feel safe?"

09

What can we do when someone reacts to the topic of the body with resistance, because church language once wounded them?

Do not try to deny the wound or to defend ourselves immediately. Rather acknowledge: "I understand that this was painful." Sometimes the first step of reconciliation is precisely the acknowledgement of the wound, not an argument.

And then look for a language that is humane. Less lecturing, more accompanying. Fewer categories, more questions. The body needs respect — and respect begins by not pushing a person through our own vocabulary.

10

How do we lead a group when the topic of the body or sexuality comes up unexpectedly and people's sensitivities differ?

First slow the pace and name that this is a sensitive topic. Then set the framework: respect, confidentiality, no ridicule and no pressure.

And then work with questions, not verdicts. The group does not need answers straight away. It needs a safe space where one can speak without shame. And that is itself already pastoral work.

11

What is the simplest way not to cause harm in this area when we teach, preach or lead a conversation?

Ask permission. It sounds simple, but it is fundamental: "May I open this topic? Is that all right for you?" In doing so, we return to people the control over their own story.

And then use a language that is concrete and does not humiliate. Less moralising, more acknowledgement that the body is a place where beauty and vulnerability meet. When that can be felt, people stop defending themselves and begin to listen.

12

How do we recognise that we have stopped talking in this area and started fighting?

By the signals our own body gives us. A tightening in the stomach or pressure in the chest may show that it is no longer about the person, but about ideological victory. Then the most theological step is often to return to the body: to slow down, take a breath and admit that a sensitive place has been touched.

Theological Reflection
Key Concept

The Pontifical Biblical Commission, in the document What Is Man?, recalls the biblical view of the human being as a unity: body and spirit are not enemies, but interconnected dimensions of one person. If, in spirituality, the body is severed from dignity, space arises for shame and manipulation.

Towards an Integrated Spirituality

The experience of accompaniment likewise shows that the topics we cannot safely name in a community come back to us in other forms: in anxiety, in a double life, in anger or in a retreat into isolation. That is also why we need to speak of the body truthfully, sensitively and in a safe environment — not from a position of control, but from a position of dignity and responsibility.

In pastoral practice, therefore, it is important to speak of sexuality in a way that keeps it connected to the dignity of the person. Texts on pastoral care for LGBT couples likewise show that the greatest need is a safe space in which a person does not have to defend themselves, but can search for inner authenticity.

The practice of reconciliation in the area of the body and intimacy does not mean abolishing norms. It means renewing human speech: speaking in such a way that the norm serves life instead of stifling it.

Protecting the person in sensitive questions of the body, intimacy and identity begins from their inalienable dignity. Dignitas infinita recalls that this dignity belongs to every human person and endures in every situation of life.

The same document also recalls that biblical anthropology is always relational: the human being is not an isolated individual, but a being called into relationship — with God, with others and with themselves. This matters for questions of intimacy too: they are not only about moral norms, but also about maturity, dignity and the ability to bear responsibility for another.

Theobald stresses that the reading of Scripture and faith learn a new language in every age — without losing their content. With sensitive topics (including sexuality), this means slowing down, not caricaturing the other side and working with what people actually experience. Research findings on attitudes and experiences can also serve pastoral work as a mirror: they help name wounds that would otherwise remain hidden, and set boundaries so that they protect the person, not an ideology.

The biblical perspective, meanwhile, protects against reducing a person to "a problem": the body, the emotions and desires are part of the calling to love and to responsibility.

When Scripture is read as a mirror rather than a hammer, it can lead a person to the truth about themselves without humiliation. In this way, pastoral care can join clarity and gentleness: naming boundaries while carrying the person in their fragility.

This chapter is therefore not about "winning a dispute". On the contrary, it is about a community maturing — through listening, through discernment and through learning from its own stories.

The Jesuit author Augustine Thomas SJ, who works on psychospiritual transformation, points out that the "true Self" does not appear by suppressing vulnerability, but by learning to carry it within a truthful relationship. The integration of identity is therefore not self-construction, but a process in which a person learns to accept their own fragility without shame.

In pastoral accompaniment, this practically means cultivating the art of listening. Publications from our own context devoted to counselling and pastoral accompaniment show that counselling is not the giving of ready-made advice, but a space in which experience is named and a new understanding is born — often more slowly than we would wish, but for that very reason more truthfully.

Key Takeaways

The Body Is a Place of Truth

  • Where there is too much shame, the truth cannot be heard. The first step is a safe conversation.
  • Understanding chastity as integrity is more healing than reducing it to control.
  • Pastoral care for people in the area of sexuality begins with recognising dignity, not with labelling.
A Pause on the Journey
Personal reflection

Which words or attitudes help me accept my own vulnerability without shame, and which close me down?

In a group

What conditions does our group need so that sensitive topics can be discussed without pressure, ridicule or oversimplification?

A small step

In the next sensitive conversation, I will first ask permission and check whether it is safe to continue with the topic.

Questions for Discernment
  1. What language do I use when I speak about the body, intimacy and sexuality — is it wounding, shaming, or healing?
  2. What stirs in me at this topic — shame, resistance, desire, uncertainty, or hope?
  3. Where in my life do values, desires and vulnerability meet?
  4. Can I distinguish between responsibility, guilt and self-hatred?
  5. What words would help us speak about the body truthfully and, at the same time, with respect?
  6. Where do I need help, accompaniment or a safe conversation?
For Groups and Facilitators
Suggested format

75-105 minutes; 6-12 people. Aim: To develop a language that, on sensitive topics, joins truthfulness, dignity, boundaries and safety.

Opening question

Which words on the topics of the body and identity open people up, and which shame or silence them?

Main activity

Language that opens and closes: the group compares anonymous sentences and rewrites them so that they remain truthful but do not humiliate the person.

Safety framework

Participants have the right not to share personal experiences. The topic does not replace therapy or professional accompaniment.

Shared discernment

What conditions do we need so that sensitive topics can be discussed without pressure and oversimplification?

Closing step

Each person chooses one sentence or communication habit with which they want to better protect the dignity of the other in sensitive topics.
