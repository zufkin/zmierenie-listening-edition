10Speaking about God

To speak about God today means beginning with the person, their experience and a language they can hear.

Reading approximately 20–30 minVideo 5:51 · optionalGroup optional

The full text is the core of the chapter. The video and group work are optional extensions.

In this chapter, you will learn to
  • distinguish witness from moralising and quick explanation
  • translate the language of faith without losing its content
  • discern when it is necessary to speak and when to remain in silence and presence.
Chapter videoOn GodVideo in Slovak

When we accompany conflict or pain, we soon come up against the question of language. How can we speak about God so that it does not sound like a cliché? And how do we avoid a way of speaking that would silence what the other is really experiencing?

One of the frequent sentences of our time is: "I don't miss God." Not as a provocation — rather as a statement of fact. Many people work on their development, grow professionally, care for their families — and religious language is distant to them. Others might even want to believe, but have no trust in the Church, or the language of faith is bound up with a wound for them.

In such an environment, one cannot speak about God in the old way, as if God were self-evident. We need a new translation — not a change of content, but a change of communication. A language that is truthful yet not aggressive. A language that carries mystery yet does not escape into fog.

This chapter rests on the question of how to speak about God in a way that is intelligible to today's person and, at the same time, faithful to the Gospel.

FROM THE ORIGINAL CONVERSATION · OPTIONAL

How to Speak about God: Language, Mysticism and Experience

A conversation about deeper layers of language, the experience of faith and the search for words that do not impose God on a person who is not looking for God. This is a personal and at times contemplative reflection, not a systematic lecture.

Dialogue

Remaining Authentic

01

Why do people today often not react to the word "God"?

Because the word has lost its content. For one person God is just a moral policeman. For another an abstraction. And for yet another a memory of manipulation.

If the language is to come alive again, it needs to reconnect with experience: with the question of meaning, with pain, with the longing for trust.

02

What is the first step when we want to speak about God in a secular environment?

Begin with the person. Not as a tactic, but as respect for the distinctiveness and uniqueness of each journey. The Gospel is always born within the story of a concrete person. That is why it is important to ask what hurts people today, what they are searching for, what they fear, where they experience goodness.

Only then does it make sense to speak about God — not as a special topic, but within the questions of meaning.

03

How do we avoid moralising?

Moralising is often anxiety in disguise. When I fear losing control over values, I begin to harden my language. But hardening does not reach people — it drives them away instead.

It helps to distinguish between witness and judgement. Witness says: "This is what I have lived, this is what gives me meaning." Judgement says: "You are bad." Witness opens; judgement closes.

04

And what when a person asks: Where was God when evil happened to me?

That is one of the hardest questions. There a quick answer will not help. Presence will. Christianity has one powerful image: God is not outside pain. God is within it — on the cross.

Sometimes the most truthful witness to God is solidarity. Language comes only afterwards.

05

What does the "translation" of faith mean?

Translation means saying the same thing, but in a way the other can hear. Not changing the content, but changing the form. And form is not secondary — form is part of the truth.

06

What do you watch out for today, so as not to say something about God that wounds people?

Most of all I watch that I say only what I have lived through, and not what merely sounds wise or what I imagine to be good for the other person. When someone is in crisis, they do not need a maxim. They need presence and a hearing.

And I watch out for moralising too. For a language that immediately judges who is "right" and who is not. That closes people from within.

07

When is it better to speak about God, and when is it better to stay with silence?

God can be spoken about when there is trust between us. When the other person feels that I do not want to correct them but to accompany them. Then even a simple sentence can be deep.

And silence is needed where trust is only just being born — or where the pain is so great that every sentence would sound like an explanation. Sometimes the most genuine theology is born where we manage to see another person's story with such understanding as if it were our own.

08

How do we translate theological words into a language that is not "churchy", so that it does not sound foreign?

Best through stories and experience. Instead of the concepts themselves, ask what the given reality does to a person; instead of definitions, notice what movement it stirs in us.

And then through simplicity. Not banality, but the simplicity that is born of maturity. That is also why I consider working on the intelligibility of language to be crucial.

09

What has proved itself when you speak about God before a mixed audience — believers, seekers and sceptics?

Begin with what is common: experience. Fear, joy, loss, the longing for meaning. And only from there seek words that can also carry theological content.

And be authentic: not pretending we have everything resolved. Today's person can often accept uncertainty, but is very sensitive to insincerity.

10

How do we react when speech about God is met with irony or open rejection?

Do not strike back. Rather stay with the person: "I understand that this is how you feel." Irony can be a defence against hurt or disappointment.

And then a question can be offered instead of an argument: "What brought you to this?" When a person reaches their story, a space sometimes opens where God can be spoken about more humanely.

11

What might a sentence sound like that we can allow ourselves almost always — without exerting pressure?

Perhaps like this: "I am here with you." When we mean it seriously, it carries more of the Gospel than grand concepts. And only afterwards, when there is room, can we look for words about God that do not close but open. That is the bridge to the question of how, in today's world, to guard our language so that it remains human.

Theological Reflection
Key Concept

The conference paper "How to Speak about God Today" shows that the problem lies not only in arguments, but in a change of culture: traditional religious frameworks are no longer self-evident.

The Authenticity of Language

Jan Loffeld names the situation of religious indifference: God is not an object of dispute, but is simply absent as a topic. Speech about God must therefore lean on the experience of meaning, trust and hope — otherwise it will remain in a closed bubble.

That is why pastoral theology stresses the need for new forms of communication: dialogue, witness, story, the ability to listen. Conferences and public events at the Faculty of Theology of Trnava University are also part of this search.

This estrangement does not arise from hostility, but because religious language has drifted away from people's experience. Tomáš Halík teaches us to approach the person patiently — not as an enemy, but as a question. Theobald reminds us that intelligibility is not a betrayal of faith, but a service: words are meant to open a space for encounter.

Pastorally this means that the problem is not only a lack of information about the faith, but also the experience of religious words being unintelligible. Speech about God cannot be renewed by speaking more, but by speaking more precisely — in contact with what people actually live.

Theobald reminds us that faith is born from listening to Scripture, but also from the encounter of Scripture with concrete experience. If we want to speak about God today, we need a language that is at once faithful and human: it does not humiliate people for their questions or ridicule them, but learns from those questions. That is also why it matters to join theological reflection with dialogue and formation — as we strive to do in the public appearances and educational programmes out of which this book grows.

If speech about God is to be convincing today, it must be humble: it should witness rather than prove, and accompany rather than judge.

In an environment of disengagement, it may be more effective to speak about God indirectly: through stories, images, the experience of goodness and forgiveness. Such a language does not hide faith, but gives it a chance to be heard even without a "religious vocabulary".

A reflection on speaking about God in a secularised environment points out that the first challenge is not technique but authenticity. The language of faith must be able to carry vulnerability: to admit where we are not certain, to name experience and not to pass off our own positions as the voice of God.

The concept of a "theology of elasticity", developed by the independent theologian Jörg Basten, helps sustain the tension between faithfulness to tradition and openness to new situations. Elasticity here does not mean relativism, but the ability to present the same Gospel intelligibly in changing contexts.

Key Takeaways

Handing On Experience

  • To speak about God today means first rediscovering the person and their story.
  • In an environment of indifference, witness, dialogue and intelligible language have a better chance of reaching people than pressure or moralising.
  • A convincing form of witness can be authentic presence: staying with a person when their sense of meaning is collapsing.
A Pause on the Journey
Personal reflection

Which words of faith do I use as clichés, and which arise from my own lived experience?

In a group

How can our community speak about God intelligibly to people who have no experience of church language, or were wounded by it?

A small step

I will choose one frequently used religious sentence and rewrite it in human speech without losing its meaning.

Questions for Discernment
  1. What images of God did I receive, and which of them help or wound today?
  2. Which words of faith do I use as clichés without contact with experience?
  3. When do I speak about God in a way that creates space, and when in a way that closes the conversation?
  4. What question is asked today by a person who no longer expects a religious answer?
  5. What would help translate the language of faith into a speech intelligible in my environment?
  6. What concrete conversation or text can I revise so that it becomes more truthful and more humane?
For Groups and Facilitators
Suggested format

75-105 minutes; 8-24 people. Aim: To practise translating theological content into a language that is truthful, humane and appropriate to a concrete experience.

Opening question

Which words of faith are intelligible in our environment, and which sound like a cliché or a ready-made answer?

Main activity

Translating the faith: the group works with short situations and seeks a sentence that is theologically truthful, humanly intelligible and appropriate to the concrete person.

Safety framework

Amid pain and loss, no quick spiritual explanations are offered. Presence and the acknowledgement of experience take priority.

Shared discernment

How can we speak about God to people who do not know church language, or were wounded by it?

Closing step

Each person rewrites one religious cliché into a sentence they can say truthfully and personally.
