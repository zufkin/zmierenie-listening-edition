08What Unites Us

Unity does not arise from uniformity, but from a return to the shared core and to a language that can bear difference.

Reading approximately 20–30 minVideo 5:46 · optionalGroup optional

The full text is the core of the chapter. The video and group work are optional extensions.

In this chapter, you will learn to
  • distinguish between the core of faith and peripheral disputes
  • read the creed as a compass, not as an instrument of exclusion
  • seek a common language that protects both the truth and human dignity.
Chapter videoLanguageVideo in Slovak

Openness needs a firm common foundation. Otherwise it can turn into weariness or chaos. In the history of the Christian faith, the creed became such a point: a simple core to which we can return when we are arguing about everything else. The 1700th anniversary of the Council of Nicaea reminds us that unity is not uniformity, but a shared "yes" to what unites us.

When a society is polarised, people often argue about peripheral questions — about words, symbols and details. Yet the problem tends to lie deeper: the shared core has been lost. Without a common foundation, even small differences turn into war.

In the history of Christianity we can find several milestones when schools of thought parted ways completely or found common ground again. The Nicene anniversary reminds us that at the core there is not merely an intellectual construct, but a person. The creed came into being as an attempt to hold together what is essential, so that the Church would not disintegrate into groups drifting ever further apart.

Many today recite the Nicene Creed without feeling its significance. Yet precisely its remembrance can point to a way of reconciliation: returning to a common language and discerning anew what is core and what is periphery.

Action in the Gospel does not begin with seizing power over another. It begins with a question that gives a person back their voice and dignity. Bartimaeus is not treated as a problem; on the contrary, he is addressed as a person who can name his own longing.

Praxeology is therefore neither activism nor a set of techniques. It is learning to act in such a way that accompaniment, speaking about God, working with technologies and hope remain a service to the person and to the common good.

Dialogue

We Can Always Find Common Ground

01

Why return to Nicaea at all? Isn't it merely a historical topic?

History matters here, but this is not nostalgia. The point is that Nicaea addressed the question of unity in a time of conflict. And we live in a time of conflict today.

Nicaea shows that the Church can conduct hard disputes — and still seek a language that makes it possible to stay together.

02

How can the creed help in today's practical conflicts?

By reminding us of what holds us together. When, in a dispute, we ask, "What is the foundation of our faith?", many side battles lose their absoluteness. This does not mean the details are unimportant. It means the relationship does not have to perish because of differences.

The creed also reminds us that Christianity is relational from the beginning: Father, Son and Spirit — a communion. And communion is the opposite of an isolated ideology.

03

But isn't the creed often used as an instrument of exclusion?

It can be — and in history it has been. That is precisely why it matters to read it not as a test of correctness, but as an orientation. A compass does not determine who is to be excluded — it helps us not lose direction without harming one another.

The practice of reconciliation does not mean abolishing boundaries. It means seeking a common language in the fact that we are human and do not want to lose one another.

04

What does the anniversary of the Council of Nicaea recall today?

Above all questions: What actually holds the Church together when the culture is changing? And how do we lead a dialogue with the world without losing our own identity? These are deeply theological questions, yet very practical ones: they touch the style of communication, power and pastoral care.

Nicaea teaches that unity cannot be manufactured by pressure. Unity is born from the search for common ground.

05

When we differ in the Church in both views and sensibilities, what can we return to so that we do not fall apart?

Not to an opinion, but to a relationship. Nicaea returns us to the faith that God became close in Christ. That is a certainty which can bear differing interpretations and tensions alike.

And the second firm point is human dignity. If dignity is lost in a debate, we have lost the Christian foundation too — even if we were leaning on the correct formulations.

06

How can all this be lived in practice, so that it does not remain just a line in the creed?

The creed is like a compass: it does not answer everything, but it helps us not lose direction when the landscape around us changes.

And then also by allowing ourselves to say: "We do not understand everything, but we do not want to split into camps." That is a very strong decision today.

07

What happens when dogma is used as a stick? And how can that be prevented?

When dogma turns into a stick, it ceases to be a confession of faith and becomes an instrument of power. Then it is no longer about God, but about control.

It can be prevented by letting the creed remain prayer and liturgy, not a weapon. And by guarding human dignity — that is a very "Nicene" criterion even today.

08

How could a small group work with the creed so that it is alive and not schoolish?

Simply: read the creed sentence by sentence and ask what it stirs in me. Where it is easy for me and where demanding. And allow ourselves questions too, not only correct answers.

Such work is not a relativising of faith. It is its maturing. And at the same time it creates a common language: even if we experience it differently, we know to whom we belong.

09

If we had to name one "Nicene gesture" for pastoral work today, what would it be?

Being able to say: "This is what holds us together — and within it there is room for us in our variety." That is not relativism, but a conscious choice not to exclude people from the community at the first difference.

Nicaea was a decision to protect the unity of faith in a time of tension. Today that often means protecting the unity of the relationship so that discernment can continue. And precisely here a space opens naturally for practical work with tension — for mediation.

Theological Reflection
Key Concept

The proceedings of the scholarly conference on the anniversary of the Council of Nicaea show the double function of that event: to protect the core and, at the same time, to enable communication. The council sought a common language meant to prevent faith from disintegrating into mutually incompatible interpretations.

The Language of Unity

Jürgen Habermas and Joseph Ratzinger, in their dialogue on reason and religion, point to the tension between secular reason and religious tradition. Their exchange shows that without a common language and without recognising the rational dimension of faith, dialogue with society breaks down into parallel monologues.

If the creed is read as a compass, it can also become a practical instrument of reconciliation: it helps us distinguish where a legitimate difference is possible and where what is common is already being broken.

The Habermas–Ratzinger dialogue is useful because it shows the possibility of a common language: reason and faith need not exclude one another if they critically examine and correct each other. This matters for reconciliation, because a community does not fall apart merely over differing opinions, but above all when it loses a language in which one can speak without caricature.

The Nicene Creed can be understood as a historical example of a language through which the Church sought to express the shared core of faith in the midst of dispute. When we ask today why even Christians polarise so quickly, Haidt's analysis of moral intuition reminds us that our "correct" feelings easily turn into an identity wall. The creed can then function as a shared prayer that reminds us to whom we belong, rather than whom we attack.

Habermas and Ratzinger both suggest, moreover, that society needs "translation": religious language should enter the public space in a form that can be argued for — and, at the same time, secular reason should learn from ethical sources it did not itself create.

The creed can today also be a "bridge": it reminds us that before we divide into camps, we stand together before God. This shared act of faith has the power to soften language and restore humility in a dispute.

When we ask what holds the Church together, we naturally return to the patristic roots. The Slovak patrologist Miloš Lichner points out that the early Christian tradition is not a museum but a school of discernment: it shows how unity is born in a time of disputes, when a common language is being sought.

The Swiss theologian Hans Urs von Balthasar reminds us that the "style" of the Church is not merely a sum of statements, but the way faith becomes visible. Unity is therefore not sustained by norms alone, but through the beauty and integrity of a life capable of bearing tension without humiliation.

That is also why it is useful to read the "language of unity" as a compass: it does not manufacture uniformity, but keeps the direction. In the Slovak context, this emphasis also appears in the Jubilee catecheses, which join the biblical and pastoral perspectives and remind us that newness is not a threat, but a call to discernment.

Key Takeaways

A Return to the Core

  • In polarised disputes, a question helps: What unites us, and what draws us off to the peripheries?
  • The creed is a compass, not an instrument for checking correctness. It is meant to guide, not to exclude.
  • Unity is born from finding what is common, not from pressure. Truth can be spoken about even with respect for a different view.
A Pause on the Journey
Personal reflection

What is the core of my faith or value conviction that I can express simply and without attack?

In a group

Which shared values or commitments can hold our community together even amid differing interpretations?

A small step

In one dispute I will deliberately separate the core of the problem from peripheral topics and propose a sentence on which we can agree.

Questions for Discernment
  1. Which topics in my environment unite on the surface but divide within?
  2. What is, for me, the core of the Christian faith or of human dignity that I can state simply?
  3. When do I use the symbols of faith as a compass, and when as a weapon?
  4. What common language would help us speak without oversimplification and attack?
  5. Whom do we need to invite to the table if we want to speak about what unites us?
  6. What step could renew the common good in our environment?
For Groups and Facilitators
Suggested format

75-105 minutes; 8-20 people. Aim: To distinguish the shared core, legitimate differences and peripheral disputes without enforcing uniformity.

Opening question

Which disputes in our environment present themselves as core, though they concern rather peripheral questions?

Main activity

The compass of the shared core: the group sorts statements into core, important differences and peripheral disputes, and then formulates a common foundation.

Safety framework

The aim is not forced agreement, but a more precise discernment of what is common and where we remain different.

Shared discernment

Which shared values or commitments can hold us together even amid differing interpretations?

Closing step

The group formulates one shared sentence that protects the core while leaving room for difference.
